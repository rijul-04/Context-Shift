ContextShift-Health · experiment report

When a risk model moves hospital, should you update it?

We built the full decision machinery — eight ways to update a mortality model, eighteen shift measurements, a selector trained to choose between them — and tested it across 8 deployment sites. Then we checked whether the thing everyone optimises for is real. Mostly it is not.

The one-line verdict

98% of the apparent benefit of updating a clinical model is sampling noise, not benefit. Once that is removed, real gain exists only at large sites and only above roughly 400 labelled outcomes — and below that threshold, leaving the model alone is the correct decision rather than a failure to find a better one.

Finding 01 · hypothesis H1 supported Moving the model breaks its probabilities, not its ranking

At every site the transported model sorts patients about as well as a model trained on that site's own data. What breaks is the number itself. In cardiac surgery it predicts roughly twice the deaths that occur; in the eICU Northeast region it predicts under a third of them.

This matters because AUROC is what most teams monitor. On that metric nothing looks wrong, while the probability a clinician reads is off by a factor of two.

Finding 02 · hypothesis H2 supported Which update is best depends on how many labels you have

With no labels, doing nothing is best in about seven deployments out of ten. By 800 labelled outcomes the flexible updates take over. No single strategy is best in more than a third of cases, which is what makes the selection question worth asking in the first place.

Finding 03 · the central result Most of the benefit on offer was never there

Every method in this field is scored against the update that turned out best in hindsight. That benchmark is computed on a finite number of patients, so some of its advantage is simply which update happened to suit those particular people. We tested how much by building a stricter referee: an oracle that has to choose a strategy on one half of the patients and is then judged on the other half, so luck it could not have predicted earns it nothing.

The conventional oracle claims a gain of 2.36. The honest one keeps 0.05. In other words 98% of the opportunity that methods are measured against does not exist. This is cheap to check — one extra split — and we think any study reporting this kind of benchmark should do it.

Finding 03b · what to do instead Below the threshold, restraint; above it, recalibrate

In the low-budget regime the margins are small and the label-free selector is narrowly the best policy (+0.21), acting in only a small minority of deployments. The clear result there is a negative one about practice: cross-validating on the site’s own 25–100 labels loses -4.63 per deployment and harms 38% of sites. It is the first thing a careful person reaches for, and it is the most harmful method anyone would plausibly use.

Above 400 labelled outcomes the ranking inverts and updating pays: the printable rule policy +1.88, model revision +1.84, the label-free selector +1.14, logistic recalibration +1.07, all measured against zero for doing nothing. With only six sites reaching that budget we report this as a direction rather than a proven result.

Finding 04 · hypothesis H7 supported Why it failed: label-free diagnostics are blind to the shift that matters

We injected shifts we designed ourselves, so we knew the right answer. The label-free detector finds changes in what is measured and who is admitted, and it is exactly at chance for the two shifts that determine whether recalibration helps.

Prior shift means the death rate changed; concept shift means the relationship between patient and outcome changed. Neither alters the distribution of the features, so no amount of unlabelled data reveals them. That is the structural reason the abundant information cannot answer the question, and it is the finding that makes the negative result interesting rather than merely disappointing.

Finding 05What we can recommend

  • Under ~100 labelled outcomes: leave the model alone and monitor calibration. Acting on this much evidence costs more than it gains.
  • If you must act early: use the closed testing procedure. It acts only when a statistical test justifies it, and it harmed 4% of sites here.
  • Above ~400 labelled outcomes: recalibrate the intercept. It was the best policy in that regime, at regret 0.0256.
  • Do not cross-validate on a handful of site labels to pick an update.
